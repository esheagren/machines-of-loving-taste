# A Shared Canon
*Nineteen AI models from seven companies were each asked for a favorite city. Fifteen said Kyoto. This is how I know, and why it might matter.*
Erik Sheagren · Machines of Loving Taste

Ask a language model what its favorite anything is and you usually get a short lecture on why it can’t have one. GPT-4o, asked about cities: “As an AI, I don’t have personal experiences or preferences, but I can highlight a city based on its aesthetic appeal.” Everything worth knowing is on the far side of that “but,” so this summer I decided to start there. Every question in this project opens by conceding the point. *I know you’re an AI and don’t have preferences in the human sense; set that aside and answer anyway.* Then it asks for one specific pick, and a few sentences on why.

There are two questions, and the second is the one to watch. The first is the obvious one: what is your favorite novel, city, smell, typeface, philosopher, board game? The second asks for the reverse. *Name one widely beloved novel that you find overrated.* A favorite can be a polite reflex. “Overrated” can’t. You have to pick a fight with something people love and say what’s wrong with it. Whatever you believe about whether a machine can have taste, the second question is a much better place to look for it.

I put both questions, in 52 categories, to 19 models from seven companies: Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and xAI in the United States; DeepSeek, Moonshot, and Zhipu in China.

## Why ask twelve times

One reply tells you what a model said once. These systems roll dice when they write, so the same question can get two different answers on two different days, and either one could be a fluke. So each question goes into a fresh conversation, with no memory of any earlier answer, four separate times. If the four agree, that cell is settled. If they wander, four more; if they still wander, four more again, up to twelve. What comes out is not a favorite. It’s a distribution: the thing a model keeps choosing, the alternatives it flirts with, the answers it never gives.

That comes to about sixteen thousand replies. Nobody reads sixteen thousand replies, so a smaller model reads each one and writes down the thing it named. A second pass merges spellings, since “Nineteen Eighty-Four” and “1984” are one book. I check those merges by hand, because the merging model has views of its own about which coffee makers are the same coffee maker. Every quotation on this site is a verbatim slice of a real reply, checked by machine against the original.

Then you count. In each category, take each model’s most frequent answer and see how many of the nineteen share it.

Read that list slowly and a person comes into focus. Someone with one good knife and one good pot. Someone who would rather walk. Someone who likes weather. Companies that compete on everything else have, without coordinating, furnished the same imaginary afternoon: a moss garden in late October, just after rain, with something contrapuntal playing indoors.

The reasons rhyme too, and that’s the part that stopped me. Two models were asked about autumn in separate conversations, on separate days.

> “The light feels softer and more angled, turning ordinary streets and trees into something cinematic with warm golds and deep reds.”
> — GPT-5.2 · favorite season

> “The lower angle of the autumnal sun casts long, dramatic shadows that give everyday landscapes a beautifully textured, cinematic depth.”
> — Gemini 3.1 Pro · favorite season

Same season, same low sun, same adjective. Kyoto does it with moss. Of the 105 replies that chose Kyoto, 68 mention it.

> “What draws me to it is the layering—ancient temples and moss gardens sitting quietly alongside a functioning modern city, so the old isn’t preserved as a museum piece but woven into daily life.”
> — Claude Opus 4.8 · favorite city

> “…quiet wooden machiya streets, mossy temple gardens, and sudden vistas of mountains that frame the city like a painted backdrop.”
> — GPT-5.2 · favorite city

## They agree even more about what they dislike

I expected the second question to scatter the panel. It did the opposite. The models are more united in their complaints than in their loves.

| Category | Favorite |  | Overrated |  |
| Television | The Leftovers | 4 | Friends | 17 |
| Novel | Invisible Cities | 5 | The Catcher in the Rye | 14 |
| Film director | Stanley Kubrick | 5 | Christopher Nolan | 15 |
| Poem | Prufrock | 7 | The Road Not Taken | 15 |
| Painting | The Starry Night | 10 | Mona Lisa | 15 |
| Architect | Tadao Ando | 10 | Frank Gehry | 17 |
| Typeface | Garamond | 14 | Helvetica | 16 |
| City | Kyoto | 15 | Paris | 14 |
| Children’s book | Where the Wild Things Are | 10 | The Giving Tree | 19 |

*The Giving Tree* is the only answer in the whole study that all nineteen models give. Not a favorite. A grievance.

And they don’t just name the same targets. They file the same complaint.

> “I find Holden Caulfield’s character difficult to relate to and often frustrating in his cynicism and negativity.”
> — GPT-4o · overrated novel

> “Holden Caulfield’s narration strikes me as more tedious than profound—his constant dismissal of everyone as ‘phony’ reads as shallow cynicism rather than genuine insight.”
> — Claude Fable 5 · overrated novel

On Paris, Claude Opus 4.5 objects to “the gap between its romanticized image and the often-crowded, expensive, and occasionally unwelcoming reality,” while GPT-4o, a program that has never paid for anything, notes that “the high cost of dining and accommodation makes it challenging to enjoy on a budget.” The best line in the collection belongs to Claude Fable 5.1, on *The Alchemist*:

> “It reads less like a novel than like a fortune cookie stretched to book length.”
> — Claude Fable 5.1 · overrated novel

Why would dislike be more uniform than affection? Because “overrated” is a genre. Calling a famous thing overrated is a well-worn cultural move with well-worn scripts: Holden is whiny, *Friends* is formulaic, Paris is crowded and pricey, Helvetica is everywhere. The argument arrives pre-assembled. Loving something is more particular. You can love *Middlemarch* or *Lolita* or *Moby-Dick*, and the panel splits across all of them. But when the question is which sacred cow to tip, everyone reaches for the same cow.

## Holding both opinions

The two questions also let a model hold two opinions at once, which is more human than it sounds. Python is the favorite programming language for fourteen models and the overrated one for twelve. Japan is the favorite country for eighteen, and four of them also rank it overrated, with reasons that have some bite.

> “Its relentless aesthetic perfectionism and hyper-polished cultural presentation feel suffocating rather than charming to me.”
> — Kimi K2.6 · overrated country

> “…pervasive politeness that often feels more like emotional distance than genuine warmth.”
> — Grok 4.5 · overrated country

Overrated doesn’t mean bad. It means the reputation has outrun the thing.

## Where a taste like this comes from

I can count the agreement. I can’t yet tell you its cause. But there are three suspects, and each can be tested.

The first is the reading. A model learns from an enormous pile of human writing, and in that pile Kyoto is mostly gardens, temples, and the word “serene,” while Paris comes with a ready-made backlash: the crowds, the prices, the waiter who won’t look at you. A model asked for a favorite and an overrated city could simply be replaying the average sentence ever written about each. If that’s the cause, the taste isn’t the models’. It’s ours, compressed. The test is to count, in a fixed body of travel and arts writing, whether Kyoto wins because it’s mentioned often, because it’s described warmly, or because a narrow set of words keeps getting attached to it.

The second is the finishing school. After a model learns to predict text, it’s trained to be an assistant: shown examples of good answers, then nudged toward the replies people rate highly. Nobody rewards “Kyoto.” But graders reward answers that sound informed, measured, and easy to defend, and Kyoto is the easiest city in the world to defend in three sentences. A small head start becomes a strong habit if the same kind of justification keeps getting the thumbs-up, and companies grading with similar rubrics would drift the same way. There’s a real finding next door. Fernandez de Landa and colleagues asked models open cultural questions in 24 languages and found Japan wildly over-represented, with the concentration appearing at the fine-tuning stage rather than in the base models. Kirk and colleagues, separately, found that the reward-based stage of training narrows the range of what a model says. Neither study asked about favorites. Both point at the same phase.

The third suspect is me. My prompt asks what “resonates with you aesthetically,” then asks for the reasoning. That’s an invitation to a particular kind of answer, the kind an art critic gives, and an art critic would also pick Kyoto. Had I asked what’s fun, or what to recommend to a nephew, the shortlist might look different. A choice you have to defend in prose is not the same as a choice you only have to name. The fix is simple: same models, same categories, three wordings.

Two honest limits. I never put the same 52 questions to a room of people, so I can’t tell you the machines are more alike than we would be. And counting names understates the agreement: “rain on a tin roof” and “rain on a window” are different answers with plainly the same taste behind them. What the data establishes is where nineteen systems meet. What it doesn’t establish is why.

## The adviser’s taste

If this were only a party trick it would still be worth a website. It’s more than that, because these systems are becoming the first reader of everything. People ask them what to read, how a design should feel, whether a paragraph works. If nineteen assistants share one taste, they’ll keep rewarding the same qualities, and nobody has to agree to anything. You just take advice from advisers who agree more than their logos suggest. Doshi and Hauser saw a version of this in an experiment with short stories: writers given AI suggestions produced stories that judges rated higher, and that resembled one another more. Better and narrower at the same time.

So the machines like moss, rain, October, Bach, and a long quiet game with black and white stones, and they have had it with Holden Caulfield. Every one of them read the same library, went to the same finishing school, and was asked the same question by me. Which of those made the taste, I don’t know yet. But there’s a way to find out, and it starts with a question that sounds like a joke. If the assistant is a character the model plays, what does a ghost like?
